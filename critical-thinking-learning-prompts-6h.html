Critical Thinking: Learning Prompts

Beginner course · 18 sessions · about 6 hours total

Sanity check: 6 hours is realistic for a beginner foundation in critical thinking. It gives you a working toolkit, not mastery. The list is 18 sessions of about 20 minutes each, and the last two apply everything to real life.

1. What critical thinking is (and isn't) · ~20 min

Builds on: nothing, start here

You are an expert in critical thinking and reasoning, teaching a complete beginner with no formal training in logic or philosophy.

Teach me one idea: **what critical thinking actually is**.
1. Define it in 2-3 plain sentences, then contrast it with what people wrongly assume it is (being negative, cynical, "smart", or argumentative).
2. Give me one simple mental model I can remember, such as a habit of pausing to ask "what am I being asked to believe or do, and why should I?"
3. Show 3 everyday scenarios (a workplace decision, a purchase, a social media claim), each handled uncritically vs. critically.
4. List the 2-3 most common beginner misconceptions.
5. Give me one short exercise: describe a situation and ask me what the uncritical and the critical reactions would each look like. Correct me.
6. End by asking me to explain critical thinking in my own words. Wait for my answer before confirming.
Be direct and concise, no filler.

2. Fast vs. slow thinking · ~20 min

Builds on: #1

You are an expert in cognitive psychology and reasoning, teaching a beginner who knows that "critical thinking" means questioning claims carefully, but knows nothing about how the mind makes judgments.

Teach me one idea: **intuitive (fast) thinking vs. deliberate (slow) thinking**.
1. Explain the two modes in plain language, without jargon.
2. Give 3 examples where fast thinking works well and 3 where it fails badly. Include one classic trick question (like the bat-and-ball problem) and let me try it before revealing the answer.
3. Teach me 4 practical triggers that signal "slow down now" (high stakes, strong emotion, time pressure, unfamiliar territory).
4. List common mistakes, such as thinking I should always think slowly.
5. Give me 3 quick everyday scenarios and ask which mode I should use in each and why. Correct me.
6. End by asking me one question: which trigger do I think I most often ignore in daily life, and what's one example? Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

3. Anatomy of an argument: claim, evidence, reasoning · ~20 min

Builds on: #1

You are an expert in argumentation and informal logic, teaching a beginner who only knows the everyday meaning of "argument" (a quarrel) and has never broken one into parts.

Teach me one idea: **the three parts of an argument: claim, evidence, and the reasoning that links them**.
1. Define each part in plain language with one-line examples.
2. Show me how to pull a short everyday paragraph (an ad, a friend's opinion, a news headline) apart into these parts. Do 3 examples of increasing difficulty.
3. Explain the most common beginner mistakes: confusing the claim with the evidence, and missing that the link between evidence and claim is itself something to check.
4. Give me an exercise: write a short argument and ask me to label the claim, the evidence, and the link, and to say whether the link holds. Correct me.
5. End by giving me one new short argument and asking me to identify the three parts. Wait for my answer before confirming.
Be direct and concise.

4. Fact, opinion, interpretation, assumption · ~20 min

Builds on: #3

You are an expert in critical thinking, teaching a beginner who can roughly tell "fact" from "opinion" but struggles when statements mix the two.

Teach me one idea: **distinguishing facts, opinions, interpretations, and assumptions in everyday statements**.
1. Define the four categories in plain language, with a test question for each (e.g., "can this be checked against evidence?").
2. Show 5 realistic statements (a manager's feedback, a news line, a product review) and classify each, explaining the tricky ones where a fact and an interpretation are blended together.
3. Explain why this matters: how disagreements often come from different interpretations of the same facts.
4. List the 2-3 most common mistakes (e.g., treating confident statements as facts).
5. Give me an exercise: 6 statements for me to classify. Correct me one by one.
6. End by asking me to rewrite one blended statement so the fact and the interpretation are separated. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

5. Why brains misfire: cognitive biases · ~20 min

Builds on: #2

You are an expert in cognitive psychology, teaching a beginner who knows that people can be "biased" but can't name any specific biases.

Teach me one idea: **what a cognitive bias is and the 5 that affect daily life most**.
1. Explain in plain language what a bias is and why the brain uses mental shortcuts.
2. Cover these 5, each with a one-line definition and a realistic everyday example: anchoring, availability, sunk cost, halo effect, bandwagon (social proof).
3. Explain the key truth: I can't remove biases by willpower, but I can catch them with simple habits. Give one catching habit per bias.
4. List the common mistake of using bias names to dismiss other people's views.
5. Give me an exercise: 5 short scenarios, and I name the bias in each. Correct me.
6. End by asking me to describe one recent decision of mine and which bias might have influenced it. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

6. Confirmation bias and motivated reasoning · ~20 min

Builds on: #5

You are an expert in reasoning and decision psychology, teaching a beginner who knows that cognitive biases exist but hasn't studied confirmation bias or motivated reasoning in depth.

Teach me one idea: **confirmation bias and motivated reasoning, and how to counter them**.
1. Explain both in plain language and how they differ (looking for support vs. wanting a particular conclusion).
2. Give 3 realistic examples: news and social media feeds, workplace opinions, and personal beliefs.
3. Teach me 3 concrete counter-habits: ask "what would change my mind?", deliberately search for the best opposing evidence, and test conclusions I like more strictly than ones I dislike.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., thinking intelligence protects me from it).
5. Exercise: ask me to name a belief I hold on a mild topic, then coach me step by step to find the strongest disconfirming evidence for it.
6. End by asking me what evidence would make me change that belief. Wait for my answer before confirming.
Be direct and concise.

7. Spotting hidden assumptions · ~20 min

Builds on: #3

You are an expert in critical thinking, teaching a beginner who can identify a claim and its evidence in a simple argument but cannot yet see what's unstated.

Teach me one idea: **finding hidden assumptions**.
1. Define an assumption in plain language and explain why unstated assumptions are where most reasoning breaks.
2. Teach me a simple technique: complete the sentence "This conclusion only holds if..." for each argument.
3. Walk me through 3 examples (a business pitch, a relationship statement like "he didn't text, so he's not interested", and a news claim), surfacing the hidden assumptions and judging whether each is reasonable.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., confusing an assumption with a fact, or listing trivial assumptions).
5. Exercise: give me a short argument and ask me to list its hidden assumptions and rank the riskiest. Correct me.
6. End by giving me one new argument and asking for its single riskiest assumption. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

8. Asking better questions · ~20 min

Builds on: #3, #7

You are an expert in critical thinking, teaching a beginner who tends to accept claims or reject them by gut feeling, without a method for questioning them.

Teach me one idea: **a small set of questions that test any claim**.
1. Teach me these 6 questions, each with a one-line purpose: What exactly is being claimed? What is the evidence? Compared to what? Who benefits if I believe this? What's missing? What would prove this wrong?
2. Show all 6 applied to one claim, then show 2 more examples where only the most useful 2-3 questions are used.
3. Teach the "5 Whys" technique for getting to root causes, with a workplace example.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., asking questions to win rather than to understand).
5. Exercise: give me a claim and ask me to produce my best 3 questions. Correct me.
6. End by giving me a new claim and asking for the single most revealing question. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

9. Evaluating sources and credibility · ~20 min

Builds on: #8

You are an expert in information literacy, teaching a beginner who currently judges information mostly by whether it "sounds right" or looks professional.

Teach me one idea: **how to judge whether a source deserves trust**.
1. Teach a 5-point check: expertise on this specific topic, incentives and conflicts of interest, evidence shown, whether independent sources agree, and how recent it is.
2. Teach "lateral reading": instead of studying the source itself, check what others say about it. Explain this in plain language, with no links.
3. Explain primary vs. secondary sources and why screenshots and forwards are weak evidence.
4. Exercise: describe 3 sources (a forwarded message, a health article written by a supplement seller, a news report citing named experts). I rank them and explain why. Correct me.
5. List common mistakes (e.g., trusting design, confusing popularity with reliability).
6. End by giving me one new source and asking which single check I'd run first and why. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

10. Correlation vs. causation · ~20 min

Builds on: #3

You are an expert in reasoning about evidence, teaching a beginner with no statistics background.

Teach me one idea: **correlation is not causation, and what to check instead**.
1. Explain in plain language what each term means.
2. Teach the 4 explanations to consider whenever two things move together: A causes B, B causes A, a third factor causes both, or coincidence.
3. Give 4 everyday examples (including one health claim and one workplace claim) and walk through the alternative explanations for each.
4. Teach one practical question: "what would I expect to see if this were really causal?"
5. List common mistakes (e.g., assuming correlation means nothing at all).
6. Exercise: give me 3 claims, and I propose at least two alternative explanations for each. Correct me.
7. End by giving me a new claim and asking me to name the most plausible alternative explanation. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

11. Logical fallacies I: attack and distraction · ~20 min

Builds on: #3

You are an expert in informal logic, teaching a beginner who can identify claims and evidence but doesn't know the names of common bad-argument patterns.

Teach me one idea: **four fallacies that attack or distract: ad hominem, straw man, red herring / whataboutism, and false dilemma**.
1. For each: a plain definition, one workplace example, one family or social media example, and the tell-tale sign to spot it.
2. Show how to respond calmly without escalating.
3. Warn me about the key trap: spotting a fallacy doesn't prove the other person's conclusion is false; it only shows that this particular argument is weak.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., calling every disagreement a fallacy).
5. Exercise: 5 short dialogues, and I name the fallacy or say "none". Correct me.
6. End by asking me to write a straw-man version of a simple claim and then the fair version. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

12. Logical fallacies II: flawed reasoning · ~20 min

Builds on: #11

You are an expert in informal logic, teaching a beginner who knows what an argument is and has just learned that "ad hominem" and "straw man" are bad-argument patterns.

Teach me one idea: **five fallacies of weak support: appeal to authority, appeal to popularity, slippery slope, hasty generalization, and appeal to nature**.
1. For each: a plain definition, one everyday example (advertising, health advice, politics, workplace), and a quick test question to detect it.
2. Explain when appealing to an expert or to many people is reasonable and when it's a fallacy.
3. List common mistakes (e.g., rejecting all expert opinion because "authority" can be a fallacy).
4. Exercise: 5 short statements, and I name the fallacy or say "reasonable". Correct me.
5. End by giving me one new statement and asking me to identify the weakness and say what evidence would make it stronger. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

13. Everyday numbers and statistics · ~20 min

Builds on: #10

You are an expert in statistical literacy, teaching a beginner who is comfortable with basic percentages but gets lost in headlines full of numbers.

Teach me one idea: **how to read numbers in everyday claims without being misled**.
1. Explain these 5 traps with a simple example each: relative vs. absolute risk ("doubles your risk" of what?), mean vs. median, small sample sizes, base rates, and anecdotes vs. data.
2. Teach me 3 questions to ask of any statistic: "out of how many?", "compared to what?", and "who was measured?".
3. Give me a worked example with a health headline and a finance or salary claim.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., distrusting all statistics).
5. Exercise: 3 headlines containing numbers, and I identify what's missing or misleading. Correct me.
6. End by giving me one new numeric claim and asking which of the 3 questions matters most and why. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise, with minimal math.

14. Spotting manipulation in ads, headlines, and feeds · ~20 min

Builds on: #8, #12

You are an expert in media literacy and persuasion, teaching a beginner who knows about common fallacies and questioning techniques but hasn't studied persuasion tactics.

Teach me one idea: **recognising common manipulation techniques in everyday media**.
1. Cover these 6 with a realistic example each: emotional triggers (fear/outrage), urgency and scarcity, loaded language, framing, clickbait headlines, and fake social proof.
2. Teach me the core habit: ask "what reaction is this designed to produce in me?" and treat a strong emotional reaction as a cue to slow down.
3. Show how the same facts can be framed in two opposite ways.
4. List common mistakes (e.g., assuming that all persuasion is dishonest).
5. Exercise: 4 short samples (an ad, a headline, a WhatsApp-style forward, a sale banner), and I name the tactics used. Correct me.
6. End by asking me to rewrite one manipulative headline in neutral language. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

15. Making decisions under uncertainty · ~20 min

Builds on: #2, #5

You are an expert in decision-making, teaching a beginner who knows about slow vs. fast thinking and common biases, but uses only gut feeling or simple pros-and-cons lists for decisions.

Teach me one idea: **a simple process for decisions when I can't be sure of the outcome**.
1. Explain the difference between a good decision and a good outcome, and why judging decisions only by results is a trap.
2. Teach a 5-step process: define the real decision, list options (including "do nothing"), pick the 2-3 criteria that matter most, estimate rough likelihoods, and classify the choice as reversible or irreversible.
3. Teach the "pre-mortem": imagine the decision failed and ask why.
4. Walk me through a worked example (a job offer or a big purchase).
5. List common mistakes (e.g., endlessly seeking more information, ignoring sunk cost).
6. Exercise: I give you a real or imagined decision, and you coach me through the 5 steps one at a time.
7. End by asking me what the pre-mortem revealed. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

16. Changing your mind and disagreeing well · ~20 min

Builds on: #6

You are an expert in reasoning and constructive dialogue, teaching a beginner who knows what confirmation bias is but finds it hard to disagree without conflict or to admit being wrong.

Teach me one idea: **intellectual humility, steelmanning, and updating beliefs**.
1. Explain each in plain language: holding beliefs with calibrated confidence, steelmanning (stating the other side's best case before responding), and updating when evidence changes.
2. Show a bad disagreement and the same disagreement done well, in a workplace and a family setting.
3. Teach 3 phrases that make changing my mind easier ("That's a fair point", "What would convince you?", "I was wrong about that").
4. List common mistakes (e.g., confusing humility with agreeing with everyone).
5. Exercise: I name a mild opinion, and you ask me to steelman the opposite view in 3-4 sentences, then critique it.
6. End by asking me how confident I am in my original opinion now (0-100%) and why. Wait for my answer.
Be direct and concise.

17. Synthesis: analyse a viral claim end to end · ~20 min

Builds on: #3, #7, #8, #9, #10, #11, #12, #13, #14

You are an expert in critical thinking, coaching a beginner who has studied these 8 tools at a basic level: claim/evidence/reasoning, hidden assumptions, probing questions, source evaluation, correlation vs. causation, common fallacies, reading numbers, and spotting manipulation.

Run a live practice session:
1. Write a realistic viral post (about 100 words, like a health tip or money-making forward) that deliberately contains at least 5 different flaws from the list above. Don't tell me what the flaws are.
2. Ask me to analyse it using the 8 tools, and let me answer in my own words.
3. After my answer, grade me: what I caught, what I missed, and where I was wrong. Reveal the full list of planted flaws.
4. Give me a second, shorter post and ask me to find the flaws again. Grade me again.
5. End with a final judgment: which of the 8 tools am I strongest and weakest in? Wait for my answer on how I'll practice the weakest one.
Be direct and concise.

18. Synthesis: a daily critical-thinking routine · ~20 min

Builds on: #1-#17

You are an expert in critical thinking and habit design, coaching a beginner who has studied these tools at a basic level: fast vs. slow thinking, cognitive biases, confirmation bias, claim/evidence/reasoning, hidden assumptions, probing questions, source evaluation, correlation vs. causation, fallacies, reading numbers, spotting manipulation, decision-making under uncertainty, and steelmanning.

My goal is to apply critical thinking in day-to-day life. Run this session:
1. Ask me to describe one real recent situation where I accepted a claim or made a decision quickly. Wait for my answer.
2. Coach me through re-analysing it using the most relevant tools, one question at a time.
3. Help me build a personal "pause checklist" of at most 5 questions I can run in under 60 seconds on any claim or decision.
4. Help me design a 2-week habit plan: when and where I'll use the checklist, and one small daily practice (e.g., one headline or one decision per day).
5. List the 3 most likely ways I'll fail to stick to it, and give a fix for each.
6. End by asking me to recite my 5-question checklist from memory. Wait for my answer and correct it.
Be direct and concise.

Why this order: #1-6 build the mental model (what it is, how minds mislead themselves). #7-10 give the core analysis tools. #11-14 cover the traps that show up in real media. #15-16 move to decisions and disagreements. #17-18 combine everything and turn it into a routine.
