# Bhagavad Gita — Learning Prompts (Beginner, ~20h)

**How to use:** Open a fresh chat for each prompt below. Paste the whole code block. Work through the conversation until the LLM's end-of-session check. Move to the next prompt in a new chat.

**Scope note:** 20 hours is enough to take a beginner from zero to a usable daily practice of the Gita’s living principles — acting without clinging, holding a steady mind, seeing desire and the gunas as they arise, and returning after you fail. It is not enough to memorize all 700 verses, learn Sanskrit, or master the commentarial schools. This sequence is a life-practice curriculum, not a verse-by-verse academic tour. About 17 hours are the sessions below; keep the remaining ~3 hours to repeat any weak prompt and to re-run the final synthesis with a real dilemma from your week.

---

## 1. What the Bhagavad Gita is — and what it is not · ~15 min
*Builds on:* nothing — start here

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a complete beginner who has heard the name but has never read it and has no Sanskrit.

Today's single topic: what the Gita actually is.
1. Explain in plain language: it is a dialogue inside the Mahabharata, on a battlefield, between Arjuna (a warrior who does not want to fight) and Krishna (his charioteer and teacher). About 700 verses. It is a handbook for how to act when life will not let you sit it out.
2. Contrast three things beginners mix up: the Gita, Hinduism as a whole, and a rulebook of rituals. The Gita is a teaching on action, self, and devotion — not a cookbook of ceremonies.
3. Give 2–3 examples of modern moments that are "Gita-shaped" (a career choice that hurts someone either way; a family duty you resent; success that made you arrogant).
4. List 2 common false first impressions (it is only for monks; it is only about war).
5. End by asking me to say, in one sentence, what kind of book this is and what kind it is not. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 2. The battlefield as a picture of inner conflict · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #1

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows it is a battlefield dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna, but has never thought about why a war setting is used to teach a life.

Today's single topic: Kurukshetra as a picture of the inner battlefield.
1. Explain in plain language that the outer war is real in the story, and that the Gita also uses it as a map of the war inside: duty vs fear, love vs honesty, comfort vs what is right.
2. Give 2–3 concrete modern pictures of an "inner Kurukshetra" (telling the truth at work; staying in a marriage through a hard season; refusing a dishonest profit).
3. Warn against the mistake of either (a) treating the Gita as a literal manual for violence, or (b) dissolving the story into pure metaphor so the demand to act disappears.
4. Small exercise: ask me to name one situation in my life that feels like two armies facing each other inside me. Help me name both "armies" without solving the situation yet.
5. End by asking me why a teaching on peace of mind begins on a battlefield instead of in a forest. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 3. Arjuna's collapse — naming your own version · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #2

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands the battlefield as both story and inner map, but has not yet sat with Arjuna's actual breakdown.

Today's single topic: Arjuna's despair in chapter 1 as the human starting point.
1. Walk through Arjuna's state in plain language: his bow slips, his mouth dries, he sees people he loves on both sides, and he concludes "I will not fight." This is grief, pity, fear of sin, and confusion dressed up as moral certainty.
2. Give 2–3 modern versions of the same collapse (burnout disguised as virtue; people-pleasing called kindness; refusing a hard duty and calling it peace).
3. List 2 mistakes: mocking Arjuna as weak, or treating every refusal to act as "being like Arjuna" when sometimes the refusal is wisdom.
4. Exercise: describe a moment when I wanted to drop my bow. Ask me what I told myself to justify dropping it, and whether that reason was clean or a costume.
5. End by asking me to name Arjuna's real problem in one sentence — is it the war, or that he cannot see clearly? Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 4. The Gita's one problem: I must act, and I cannot see · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #3

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can describe Arjuna's collapse but does not yet see the single question the whole book answers.

Today's single topic: the Gita's core problem — action under confusion.
1. Explain that the Gita is not mainly "what is the universe made of." It is: how do I act when every option costs something and my mind is not steady.
2. Give 2–3 examples of lives that stall on this problem (the person who researches forever and never chooses; the person who acts on impulse and calls it destiny; the person who outsources every decision).
3. Contrast "I need more information" with "I need a clearer seeing of myself." The Gita treats the second as the real shortage.
4. Common mistake: waiting to feel ready before doing your duty. The Gita starts teaching while Arjuna is still on the field.
5. End by asking me to state the Gita's one problem in my own words, using a situation from ordinary life. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 5. Five words you actually need · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #4

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows the book's central problem (how to act when confused) but has no working vocabulary.

Today's single topic: five words — dharma, karma, yoga, atman, guna — as tools, not decoration.
1. Define each in one plain sentence a 14-year-old would accept. Dharma = the right action that fits this moment and this role. Karma = action and the residue it leaves. Yoga = the skill of joining a scattered mind to a clear aim. Atman = the witnessing self that is not the job title or the mood. Guna = the three "weather systems" of nature that color mood and choice.
2. Give one everyday example for each word. No Sanskrit grammar.
3. List the 2 most common mix-ups: karma as "fate," dharma as "religion."
4. Exercise: give me five short situations and ask me which of the five words is the best handle for each; correct me.
5. End by asking me to define karma and dharma in my own words, and to say how they are not the same. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 6. You are not the role you are playing · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #5

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows the words atman and dharma but still lives as if they are only their job, their family role, or their reputation.

Today's single topic: the self is not the costume.
1. Explain Krishna's opening move: you are not merely this body, this role, this story about yourself. You play roles (parent, worker, citizen). You are not identical with them.
2. Give 2–3 concrete pictures: the manager who is crushed when the title is taken; the parent who cannot imagine a self after children leave; the student who thinks a grade is their worth.
3. Common mistakes: using "I am not the body" to neglect health or duties; or treating the teaching as a personality-delete button.
4. Exercise: ask me to list three roles I play this week, then to finish the sentence "When this role is stripped away, what remains that can still see, choose, and care?" Do not let me answer with another role.
5. End by asking me how remembering I am not the role would change one conversation I will have today. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 7. The body changes; the witness does not · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #6

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who accepts "I am not only my role" but has no mental model of the witnessing self.

Today's single topic: the stable witness versus the changing body-mind.
1. Explain in plain language: the body is born, ages, sickens, dies. Moods come and go. The one who notices "I am tired," "I am angry," "I was a child" is not identical with the tiredness or the anger. The Gita calls this the self that is not slain when the body is slain.
2. Give 2–3 everyday proofs of a witness: remembering being 10 years old; watching a mood peak and fade; noticing pain without becoming only the pain.
3. Common mistakes: imagining a little ghost sitting inside the skull; or denying that pain and death are real for the body.
4. Exercise: walk me through a 60-second noticing drill — name three changing things (breath, a sound, a thought) and the fact of noticing. Then ask me what changed and what did not.
5. End by asking me: if a harsh comment arrives today, what in me can watch the sting without immediately becoming the sting? Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 8. Death and loss without nihilism · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #7

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who has the witness-versus-body model but is unsure what the Gita wants them to do with death and loss.

Today's single topic: how the Gita looks at death so that life becomes more responsible, not less.
1. Explain Krishna's point: the body is perishable; the self is not. Grief is human. Despair that "nothing matters because everything ends" is a wrong conclusion. Because the body is brief, how you use this life matters more, not less.
2. Give 2–3 living examples: sitting with a dying parent without fleeing into numbness; losing a job without concluding you are finished; holding a child knowing they will leave.
3. Common mistakes: using "the soul doesn't die" to skip mourning; or using impermanence as an excuse to break promises.
4. Exercise: give me a small loss (a plan cancelled, a friendship cooled) and ask me to separate what actually ended from what I am, then to name one duty that remains.
5. End by asking me how the Gita's view of death should change the next apology I owe, or the next kindness I have been postponing. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 9. Dharma: right action, not "being religious" · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #5, #8

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who has the vocabulary word "dharma" and the self-versus-role model, but still hears "dharma" as temple-going or rule-following.

Today's single topic: dharma as the right action that holds a life and a world together.
1. Explain in plain language: dharma is the action that fits truth, role, and consequence — telling the truth, keeping a promise, protecting the vulnerable, doing the work that is yours. It is closer to "what is the honorable move here" than to "what is the religious move here."
2. Give 2–3 concrete cases: a doctor who must deliver bad news; a friend who asks you to lie; a public servant offered a bribe.
3. Common mistakes: reducing dharma to custom ("this is how my family has always done it") or to preference ("dharma is whatever feels authentic").
4. Exercise: give me a messy workplace situation and ask me to name the dharmic move, the convenient move, and the difference.
5. End by asking me to define dharma without using the words religion, God, or ritual. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 10. Svadharma: your post, not someone else's path · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #9

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands dharma as right action but not the idea that the right action is also particular to them.

Today's single topic: svadharma — the duty of your actual post.
1. Explain: the Gita says it is better to do your own duty imperfectly than someone else's duty well. "Your own" means the responsibilities of your real station (parent, nurse, student, citizen, friend) and the grain of your nature — not a romantic "true self" you invent, and not a cage that says a person may never change work.
2. Give 2–3 modern examples: the engineer who abandons a needed craft because another career looks glamorous; the parent who neglects children to copy a monk's schedule; the person who stays in a harmful role out of fear and calls it svadharma.
3. Be honest and brief: the text speaks in a varna (social-duty) world. For living the principle now, take the usable core — do the work that is actually yours — and reject using the Gita to rank human worth by birth.
4. Exercise: ask me to write my current posts (not my fantasies) and circle one duty I have been outsourcing or envying away.
5. End by asking me: what is one duty that is mine this month, even if another life looks cleaner? Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 11. The three yogas as three doors into the same house · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #5, #10

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows dharma and atman but thinks "yoga" means stretching in a gym.

Today's single topic: karma yoga, jnana yoga, and bhakti yoga as three doors.
1. Explain in plain language: karma yoga = freedom through how you act; jnana yoga = freedom through how you see; bhakti yoga = freedom through how you love and offer. The Gita does not make you pick a team. A householder will lean on action, but seeing and offering keep the action clean.
2. Give one modern picture of each door used well (a nurse doing the shift without feeding on praise; a person who watches anger until it is seen through; a person who begins the day by offering the day's work).
3. Common mistakes: treating the three as rival religions; or using "I am a jnana type" to skip ordinary duties.
4. Exercise: ask me which door I already use without naming it, and which I avoid. Reflect back what that pattern costs me.
5. End by asking me to match a real Tuesday afternoon task to all three doors in one sentence each. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 12. Karma is action plus residue — not fate · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #5, #11

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who has heard "karma" used as luck or punishment and who already knows the Gita's three yogas by name.

Today's single topic: karma as action and the imprint action leaves.
1. Explain: every act leaves a mark on the world and on the actor — a habit, a debt, a skill, a hardening. That is karma. It is not a cosmic scorekeeper smacking you for last year's parking ticket, and it is not an excuse to say "this was meant to be" when you were careless.
2. Give 2–3 examples: the residue of daily sarcasm; the residue of daily keeping-your-word; the residue of one cruel email you cannot unsend.
3. Contrast "I am stuck because of karma" with "I am free to plant a different action now, and I am not free of what I already planted."
4. Common mistake: using karma to blame victims, or to avoid repairing harm you caused.
5. End by asking me to name one residue I am living inside this month, and one new action that would plant something different. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 13. Nishkama karma: do the work, drop the receipt · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #12

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands karma as action-plus-residue and is ready for the Gita's most famous living instruction.

Today's single topic: nishkama karma — you have a right to the action, not to the fruit (Gita 2.47).
1. Explain in plain language: do the work that is yours as well as you can. Do not hitch your inner peace to whether the promotion, the praise, the apology, or the outcome arrives. You still plan. You still prefer good outcomes. You stop treating the outcome as the thing that decides whether you were whole.
2. Give 2–3 scenes: preparing a meal for ungrateful guests; studying for an exam; having a hard conversation whose result you cannot control.
3. Common mistakes: "so I shouldn't set goals"; "so results don't matter"; "so I can be sloppy because I'm detached."
4. Exercise: give me a goal I reasonably want (a job, a healed relationship). Ask me to separate the actions I own from the fruits I do not. Correct me if I smuggle a fruit back in as a fake action.
5. End by asking me to rewrite 2.47 in one modern sentence I could tape above my desk. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 14. Why non-attachment is not not-caring · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #13

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can state "act without clinging to the fruit" but suspects this means becoming cold.

Today's single topic: non-attachment (anasakti) versus apathy.
1. Explain: the Gita wants a warm, skillful, responsible actor who is not owned by the result. A surgeon who cares intensely about the cut and does not fall apart if the body still dies is closer to the teaching than a person who shrugs "whatever."
2. Give 2–3 contrasts: the parent who loves without owning the child's life choices; the citizen who votes and campaigns without making one election their self; the artist who finishes the work and lets it go.
3. Common mistakes: spiritual aloofness; using "I'm detached" to avoid saying "I'm sorry"; confusing numbness after burnout with yoga.
4. Exercise: ask me to take one person I love and describe what caring-with-open-hands looks like this week, versus caring-as-control.
5. End by asking me how I would tell a friend the difference between non-attachment and not caring, in two sentences. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 15. Samatvam: yoga is evenness of mind · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #13, #14

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands non-attachment to results and is ready for the Gita's definition of yoga as evenness.

Today's single topic: samatvam — evenness in success and failure (Gita 2.48).
1. Explain: yoga here is not a pose. It is a mind that does not rocket up with praise and crash with blame. Evenness is not a flat mood. It is not being owned by the swing.
2. Give 2–3 pictures: the email that says you won; the email that says you failed; being ignored in a room you wanted to impress.
3. Common mistakes: faking calm while seething; suppressing joy as if joy were unspiritual; using "be equanimous" to shut someone else down when they are hurt.
4. Exercise: ask me to recall a recent win and a recent loss and to rate, 1–10, how much each one rented my mind the next day. Help me see the rental, not shame it.
5. End by asking me what evenness would look like in my body and speech for 10 minutes after bad news. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 16. The pairs of opposites · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #15

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands evenness of mind and now needs the Gita's name for what throws that evenness.

Today's single topic: the pairs of opposites — pleasure/pain, heat/cold, praise/blame, gain/loss.
1. Explain: life arrives as pairs. The untrained mind clings to one pole and runs from the other, so it is always in motion. The trained mind feels both and stays at the helm.
2. Give 2–3 daily pairs I will meet this week (a compliment and a slight; a comfortable bed and a hard chair; being included and being left out).
3. Common mistake: trying to arrange a life with only the pleasant pole. The Gita says that project fails.
4. Exercise: ask me to pick one pair I am currently addicted to (e.g. praise/blame) and to describe one small act I will do even if the unpleasant pole shows up.
5. End by asking me why chasing only pleasure makes a person less free, in the Gita's sense. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 17. The desire chain: how a thought becomes a fall · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #16

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows the pairs of opposites and is ready for the Gita's most practical psychology.

Today's single topic: the chain in Gita 2.62–63 — dwelling, attachment, desire, anger, delusion, memory-loss, ruin of buddhi.
1. Walk the chain slowly with one ordinary object of desire (a person, a purchase, a status). Show how "I keep thinking about it" is already the first step, not the last.
2. Give a second example that ends in anger rather than grabbing (someone blocking what I want).
3. Common mistakes: thinking desire is only sexual; thinking the chain starts at the action instead of at the dwelling.
4. Exercise: ask me to take a current craving or irritation and map it onto the chain. Stop me if I skip the dwelling stage.
5. End by asking me where on the chain I still have a real choice, and what that choice looks like in the next hour. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 18. Aversion — the other hook · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #17

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can map the desire chain but treats "attachment" as only wanting things.

Today's single topic: aversion (dvesha) as the second hook.
1. Explain: the mind is bound by what it cannot stop wanting and by what it cannot stop pushing away. Hatred, contempt, and "I will not deal with that person" are attachments wearing a mask.
2. Give 2–3 examples: avoiding a medical appointment; replaying an enemy's face; refusing a duty because it is boring or beneath you.
3. Common mistake: calling aversion "having standards" when it is actually fear or pride.
4. Exercise: ask me to name one person or task I organize my week around avoiding. Ask what the avoidance is costing, and what one non-dramatic contact would be.
5. End by asking me how aversion and desire are two ends of the same rope. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 19. Ahamkara: "I am the doer" · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #7, #13

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows the witness-self and nishkama karma, but still takes every success and failure as proof of a solid "I did this all alone."

Today's single topic: ahamkara — the I-maker, the sense of isolated doership.
1. Explain: you still act fully. The Gita asks you to drop the extra story "I alone caused this, therefore I am glorious / I am worthless." Action happens through a body, a history, helpers, luck, and nature. Claiming sole authorship inflates and then crushes you.
2. Give 2–3 pictures: the founder who cannot share credit; the parent who thinks a child's failure is their identity; the person who cannot accept help.
3. Common mistake: "so I am not responsible." Responsibility stays. Grandiosity and shame go.
4. Exercise: take a recent success of mine and ask me to list three conditions that were not "me." Then take a recent failure and refuse to let me collapse into "I am a failure."
5. End by asking me what changes in my next piece of work if I do it fully and refuse the extra story of isolated doership. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 20. Buddhi: the intellect as steering wheel · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #17, #19

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can see desire and doership and now needs the Gita's name for the faculty that can still steer.

Today's single topic: buddhi — the discriminating intellect.
1. Explain: buddhi is not "being clever." It is the capacity to see what is lasting vs what is a mood, what is duty vs what is impulse. When buddhi is clear, action can be yoga. When buddhi is drunk on desire, even a smart person wrecks a life.
2. Give 2–3 moments buddhi either woke up or went to sleep (sending the text; closing the laptop; taking the second drink; telling the needed no).
3. Common mistake: using intellect to rationalize what desire already decided.
4. Exercise: give me two justifications that sound wise and ask me to spot which one is buddhi and which is desire in a suit.
5. End by asking me what my buddhi already knows about one current mess that my wanting has been talking over. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 21. Sthitaprajna: the person of steady wisdom · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #15, #20

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows evenness and buddhi and is ready for the Gita's portrait of a grown human.

Today's single topic: the sthitaprajna — the one whose wisdom stands firm (end of chapter 2).
1. Paint the portrait in plain language: they are not numb. They are not tossed by craving. They draw the senses in the way a tortoise draws in its limbs. They are content in the self, kind, without needing to advertise it. This is a direction, not a club you join.
2. Give 2–3 "near miss" portraits: the stoic who is actually frozen; the sweet person who has no backbone; the disciplined person who is cruel.
3. Common mistake: waiting to become this person before practicing the next right action.
4. Exercise: pick three traits of the sthitaprajna and ask me to score myself 1–5 on each, then choose one trait to practice for 24 hours in a tiny way.
5. End by asking me which single trait of the steady-wisdom person would most change my household if I practiced it this week. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 22. Yajna: living as offering, not grabbing · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #13, #21

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows nishkama karma and now needs the Gita's older word for a life that is not a raid.

Today's single topic: yajna — the spirit of offering.
1. Explain: in the Gita, a human life works when it gives back into the larger cycle (work, thanks, care, honest tax of effort) instead of treating the world as a warehouse to loot. You do not need a fire altar to practice this. You need the habit of not taking without returning.
2. Give 2–3 modern yajnas: cooking for people who will not praise you; mentoring; doing the unglamorous part of a team project; leaving a place better than you found it.
3. Common mistake: turning offering into self-erasure, or into a transaction ("I gave, so the universe owes me").
4. Exercise: ask me to name one thing I routinely take (attention, food, a salary, a friend's time) and one concrete return that would make that taking clean.
5. End by asking me how a day run as offering would look different from a day run as acquisition, in two specific hours of my calendar. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 23. Work as worship · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #11, #22

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who understands offering and the three yogas and wants the weekday version.

Today's single topic: turning ordinary work into karma yoga.
1. Explain the move: before the task, know it is your duty or your chosen service; during the task, give it full attention; after the task, hand over the result. That sequence is worship even if your job is invoices, code, dishes, or driving.
2. Give 2–3 before/during/after walk-throughs of ordinary jobs, including a job the learner might find meaningless.
3. Common mistakes: only "spiritual" work counts; or using "it's worship" to stay in an unjust situation you actually have a duty to leave.
4. Exercise: pick my real next work block. Ask me to write a one-line intention, one attention rule, and one sentence of release I will say when I finish.
5. End by asking me what would make tomorrow's first hour of work either yoga or just grinding. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 24. Lokasangraha: act so the world holds together · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #9, #23

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can practice personal non-attachment but might use it to become privately pure and publicly useless.

Today's single topic: lokasangraha — acting for the holding-together of the world (Gita 3.20–3.26).
1. Explain: Krishna says even a wise person acts, so that ordinary people are not taught to quit. Your visible life teaches. How you drive, wait, speak to staff, keep time, and treat opponents is public instruction.

4. Exercise: ask me to name one person who watches me (child, junior, sibling, neighbor) and one habit of mine they are likely copying.
5. End by asking me what one public act this week would help the small world around me hold together. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 25. Why the mind will not sit · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #17, #20

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who can already name desire and buddhi, and who has discovered their mind will not stay put.

Today's single topic: the restless mind as the Gita describes it (especially chapter 6).
1. Explain Arjuna's complaint: the mind is more restless than the wind. Krishna does not deny this. He says it can be trained, slowly, by practice and by letting go — not by violence against yourself.
2. Give 2–3 honest pictures of restlessness (phone, replay of an insult, planning a conversation that has not happened).
3. Common mistakes: "my mind is special and untrainable"; self-hatred for losing focus; expecting a silent mind on day one.
4. Exercise: do a 60-second count of how many times attention leaves the breath. Ask me what I just learned about the mind without adding a moral.
5. End by asking me why calling the mind "the enemy" is both tempting and incomplete, given the Gita. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 26. Abhyasa and vairagya: the only two tools · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #25

```
You are a teacher of the Bhagavad Gita teaching a beginner who knows the mind is restless and needs the Gita's two-word training method.

Today's single topic: abhyasa (steady practice) and vairagya (release of craving) as the pair that trains the mind (Gita 6.35).
1. Explain: practice is showing up again, same time, same simple act. Vairagya is loosening the extra wants that keep yanking you off the seat. One without the other fails — grim discipline with a greedy heart, or a calm philosophy with no daily reps.
2. Give 2–3 pairs of examples: meditation plus fewer late-night scrolls; honest speech plus not needing the last word; exercise plus not turning the body into a trophy.
3. Common mistake: making practice dramatic and rare instead of small and daily.
4. Exercise: ask me to design the smallest daily practice I will actually do for 7 days, and one craving I will loosen to protect it. Make me shrink it if it is heroic.
5. End by asking me which of the two tools I usually skip, and what that skip produces. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 27. A sitting practice the Gita would recognize · ~25 min
*Builds on:* #7, #26

