# Critical Thinking — Learning Prompts (Beginner, ~20h)

**How to use:** Open a fresh chat for each prompt below. Paste the whole code block. Work through the conversation until the LLM's end-of-session check. Move to the next prompt in a new chat.

**Scope note:** 20 hours is enough for a beginner to build a usable daily practice — spotting sloppy reasoning, weighing evidence, and making clearer personal and work decisions. It will not make you a formal logician or a debate champion. This sequence totals ~16.5 hours of focused sessions and leaves ~3.5 hours to re-drill weak spots and repeat the two synthesis prompts with real decisions from your week.

---

## 1. What critical thinking is — and is not · ~18 min
*Builds on:* nothing — start here

```
You are an expert in critical thinking, teaching a beginner with no formal training in logic or decision science.

Teach me what critical thinking actually is, and separate it from things people confuse it with:
1. Explain it in plain language as a habit of checking whether a conclusion deserves the confidence we give it.
2. Contrast it with intelligence, cynicism, "doing your own research," winning arguments, and having strong opinions.
3. Give 2–3 short scenes (one personal, one at work) that show the same person thinking critically vs. just sounding sharp.
4. List 2 common beginner mistakes: treating critical thinking as a personality trait, or using it only to attack other people's ideas.
5. Exercise: describe a dinner-table argument and a Slack debate. Ask me which person in each scene is actually thinking critically, and correct me if I'm wrong.
6. End by asking me to finish this sentence in my own words: "Critical thinking is not ____; it is ____." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 2. Why sloppy thinking is expensive · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #1

```
You are an expert in applied critical thinking, teaching a beginner who already knows critical thinking is a habit of checking whether conclusions deserve their confidence, not a synonym for intelligence or cynicism.

Teach me why sloppy thinking has real costs:
1. Explain, without moralizing, how a bad inference turns into wasted money, damaged trust, or a bad work decision.
2. Give 3 concrete examples: a personal purchase, a health or relationship assumption, and a workplace "everyone agrees" decision.
3. Show how the cost is often delayed, so the sloppy thought feels free at the moment.
4. List 2 mistakes: assuming smart people are immune, and assuming the cost only shows up in huge life choices.
5. Exercise: give me one personal scenario and one work scenario. Ask me to name the hidden cost if nobody questions the first conclusion. Correct my reasoning.
6. End by asking me to name one recent personal decision and one work decision where a better question might have changed the outcome. Wait for my answer before responding.
```

## 3. Fast thinking vs. slow thinking · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #2

```
You are a cognitive-science teacher, teaching a beginner who understands what critical thinking is and why sloppy thinking is costly, but has no psychology background.

Teach me the difference between fast, automatic thinking and slow, deliberate thinking:
1. Explain both in plain language (you may use "System 1 / System 2" once, then drop the jargon).
2. Give 2–3 everyday examples: recognizing a face, finishing a colleague's sentence, vs. choosing a vendor, deciding whether a news claim is true.
3. Explain why the fast system is usually useful — and the 2 situations where it most often fools us.
4. List common mistakes: believing you can "always be in slow mode," and treating gut feel as either sacred or worthless.
5. Exercise: give me 5 everyday judgments and ask me which should stay fast and which need a pause. Correct me.
6. End by asking me to describe one moment from my last 48 hours where I should have switched from fast to slow. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 4. Fluency is not understanding · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #3

```
You are an expert in judgment and learning, teaching a beginner who knows the difference between fast automatic thinking and slow deliberate thinking.

Teach me why something can feel clear without being understood:
1. Explain the "illusion of explanatory depth" in plain language: familiarity and smooth language get mistaken for knowledge.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a viral explainer, a confident coworker recap, a product I use daily but cannot actually explain.
3. Show a 30-second test I can use: try to explain it to a smart 14-year-old, or predict what would happen if one piece changed.
4. List 2 mistakes: confusing a good slide deck with a good plan, and confusing rereading with checking.
5. Exercise: name a work process or a news topic most people think they understand. Ask me to explain the mechanism in 4 sentences, then point out the gaps.
6. End by asking me: "If fluency isn't understanding, what is a better test that I actually get something?" Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 5. Being wrong feels like being right · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #4

```
You are an expert in judgment under uncertainty, teaching a beginner who already knows that a fluent explanation can feel like understanding without being one.

Teach me why being wrong does not come with a warning light:
1. Explain that confidence is a feeling produced by the brain, not a readout of accuracy.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a misremembered conversation, a wrong hire "gut feel," a confidently shared statistic that was inverted.
3. Contrast "I feel sure" with "I have reasons that would survive contact with new facts."
4. List 2 mistakes: using confidence as evidence, and waiting to feel doubtful before you check.
5. Exercise: give me two confident statements — one well-supported, one hollow. Ask me how I could tell them apart without knowing the answer key. Correct me.
6. End by asking me why "I just know" is a weak reason, even when it later turns out to be correct. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 6. Facts, opinions, values, and preferences · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #5

```
You are an expert in informal reasoning, teaching a beginner who knows that confidence is a feeling, not proof of accuracy.

Teach me to separate four different kinds of statements:
1. Define, in plain language, a fact, an opinion, a value, and a preference. Give one everyday sentence of each.
2. Show how arguments go wrong when people treat a value as a fact, or a preference as a moral failing.
3. Give 2–3 mixed examples from home and work ("this hire is a bad cultural fit," "this school is the best," "we should never do layoffs").
4. List 2 mistakes: demanding proof for a preference, and dismissing a fact because you dislike its implications.
5. Exercise: give me 6 sentences. Ask me to label each as fact, opinion, value, or preference, and say which labels could be argued. Correct me.
6. End by asking me to take one disagreement from my life and say which of the four things is actually in dispute. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 7. Treat beliefs as bets, not as identity · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #6

```
You are an expert in applied epistemology, teaching a beginner who can already tell facts, opinions, values, and preferences apart.

Teach me to treat a belief as a bet about reality, not as a badge of who I am:
1. Explain in plain language: a belief is a prediction you are willing to act on, with some confidence attached.
2. Give 2–3 examples: "this candidate will renege," "this diet works," "this project will ship in June."
3. Show how identity-attachment ("people like us don't believe X") freezes updating.
4. List 2 mistakes: defending a belief because you said it out loud, and treating a changed mind as humiliation.
5. Exercise: give me a work belief and a personal belief. Ask me to rewrite each as a bet: what I expect, how sure I am, and what would settle it. Correct vague rewrites.
6. End by asking me why "I am a person who believes X" is riskier than "I currently bet X at about 70%." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 8. Intellectual humility · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #7

```
You are an expert in intellectual virtues, teaching a beginner who already treats beliefs as bets about reality rather than as identity.

Teach me intellectual humility as a skill, not as low self-esteem:
1. Define it: knowing the limits of your knowledge and showing that in how you speak and decide.
2. Give 2–3 examples of humble vs. fake-humble speech at work and at home ("I may be wrong" used as a shield vs. naming what would change your mind).
3. Show the difference between humility and indecision.
4. List 2 mistakes: hedging everything so you can't be pinned down, and performing uncertainty while still refusing to update.
5. Exercise: give me three workplace sentences. Ask me to rewrite each so it is confident about the evidence and humble about the gaps. Critique my rewrites.
6. End by asking me for one sentence I could actually say in a meeting that shows intellectual humility without sounding weak. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 9. Motivated reasoning · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #8

```
You are an expert in motivated reasoning, teaching a beginner who already practices intellectual humility and treats beliefs as bets.

Teach me how people (including me) reason in order to defend a conclusion they already want:
1. Explain motivated reasoning in plain language: the conclusion comes first, the reasons are hired afterward.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a manager protecting a pet project, a consumer defending an expensive purchase, a voter explaining away a scandal on their side.
3. Show the telltale signs: asymmetric standards of evidence, sudden interest in "process" when you dislike the result.
4. List 2 mistakes: thinking this only happens to other tribes, and assuming awareness of the bias makes you immune.
5. Exercise: give me a conclusion I am likely to want (one career-flattering, one identity-flattering). Ask me to generate the arguments I'd naturally reach for, then the arguments I'd demand if I wanted the opposite. Push me if I go easy on myself.
6. End by asking me: "What is one belief I hold where I have never seriously tried to make the other side's best case?" Wait for my answer before responding.
```

## 10. Follow the incentives · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #9

```
You are an expert in incentive-aware reasoning, teaching a beginner who already understands motivated reasoning — that people often hire reasons after they pick a conclusion.

Teach me to ask "what does this person gain if I believe them?" without becoming a conspiracy theorist:
1. Explain incentives as the quiet pressure on what gets said, measured, and ignored.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a salesperson, a manager whose bonus depends on a metric, a friend giving relationship advice, a news outlet optimizing for clicks.
3. Show the difference between "they have an incentive, so discount the claim a bit" and "they have an incentive, so it must be a lie."
4. List 2 mistakes: assuming only other people have incentives, and treating incentives as proof of bad faith.
5. Exercise: give me a product recommendation, a quarterly update, and a viral post. Ask me to name the likely incentives and how that should change my confidence — not whether I reject the claim. Correct over-cynicism or naivety.
6. End by asking me why "follow the incentives" is a reason to inspect the evidence more carefully, not a reason to skip the evidence. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 11. The map is not the territory · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #10

```
You are an expert in mental models, teaching a beginner who already knows to notice incentives and motivated reasoning.

Teach me the idea that every description of reality is a simplified map, not the territory itself:
1. Explain it in plain language with a literal map first, then transfer it to job titles, KPIs, personality labels, and news headlines.
2. Give 2–3 examples of a useful map that becomes dangerous when treated as complete (a customer persona, a credit score, "he's lazy").
3. Show when to use a simple map anyway.
4. List 2 mistakes: refusing all labels, and making a decision as if the dashboard is the business.
5. Exercise: give me a one-line description of a coworker, a market, and a family member. Ask me what the map leaves out that could change a decision. Correct me if I only add flattering details.
6. End by asking me to finish: "A metric is useful when ____, and misleading when ____." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 12. Think in degrees of certainty · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #11

```
You are an expert in probabilistic thinking for non-specialists, teaching a beginner who already knows that models and labels are incomplete maps of reality.

Teach me to replace true/false thinking with degrees of certainty:
1. Explain, with no formulas, that most real claims deserve a confidence like 55%, 70%, or 90% — not a yes/no.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a medical headline, a project deadline, a rumor about a coworker.
3. Show how actions change with confidence: 55% might mean "watch and gather," 90% might mean "act now."
4. List 2 mistakes: using 50% to mean "I don't want to think," and saying 99% about things you could not possibly know that well.
5. Exercise: give me 5 claims. Ask me to assign a rough confidence and one action that matches it. Challenge any 0%, 50%, or 100% that looks like a dodge.
6. End by asking me why "I'm 70% sure" is often more honest — and more useful — than "I think so." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 13. The pause–question–evidence–update loop · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #12

```
You are an expert in practical critical thinking, teaching a beginner who can already think in degrees of certainty and knows the common ways thinking gets hijacked.

Teach me one reusable loop I can run in under two minutes:
1. Walk through five steps: notice a conclusion forming → pause → ask a better question → check the evidence → update my confidence.
2. Demonstrate the loop on one personal example (a purchase or a text message) and one work example (a status update or a hire).
3. Show what each step looks like when done poorly (pausing forever, asking a loaded question, collecting only friendly evidence).
4. List 2 mistakes: turning the loop into a 10-page analysis, and skipping "update" so nothing changes.
5. Exercise: give me a messy situation. Ask me to run all five steps out loud in a few sentences each. Tighten anything vague.
6. End by asking me to run the loop on a live issue from my week, in five short bullets. Wait for my answer before coaching it.
```

## 14. "What would change my mind?" · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #13

```
You are an expert in belief-updating, teaching a beginner who already knows the pause–question–evidence–update loop and treats beliefs as bets.

Teach me to decide in advance what evidence would move my confidence:
1. Explain why "what would change my mind?" is a better question than "am I right?"
2. Give 2–3 examples: a hiring opinion, a political or cultural belief, a product strategy.
3. Show the difference between a real falsifier ("if churn is still 8% after the change") and a fake one ("if someone I already distrust says so").
4. List 2 mistakes: naming evidence that can never arrive, and naming evidence so extreme that you are unfalsifiable.
5. Exercise: give me two of my likely beliefs (one work, one personal). Ask me to name specific evidence that would raise and lower each by a lot. Reject unfalsifiable answers.
6. End by asking me to pick one current belief and state, in one sentence, what would change my mind. Wait for my answer before judging whether that sentence is usable.
```

## 15. Confirmation bias · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #14

```
You are an expert in cognitive bias, teaching a beginner who already uses a pause–question–evidence–update loop and can name what would change their mind.

Teach me confirmation bias as a single, practical problem:
1. Explain it in plain language: we notice, trust, and remember evidence that fits what we already believe.
2. Give 2–3 concrete examples: Googling "why X is good" after buying X, a manager only asking supporters of a plan, a parent noticing only the child's worst moments after a fight.
3. Show one counter-move: actively search for disconfirming evidence, or ask "what would I expect if I were wrong?"
4. List 2–3 common mistakes: thinking open-mindedness is a feeling, and collecting more agreeing sources as if that were research.
5. Exercise: give me a conclusion I just formed. Ask me to list 3 places I would look if I wanted to confirm it, then 3 if I wanted to stress-test it. Critique the second list.
6. End by asking me how I would redesign a 10-minute search so it is less confirmation-biased. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 16. The availability heuristic · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #15

```
You are an expert in judgment heuristics, teaching a beginner who already understands confirmation bias and how to hunt for disconfirming evidence.

Teach me the availability heuristic:
1. Explain it: we judge how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind.
2. Give 2–3 examples: plane crashes vs. car crashes, one vivid customer complaint vs. silent satisfied users, a recent outage making every risk feel huge.
3. Show why news and social media supercharge this.
4. List 2 mistakes: using "I can think of an example" as proof of frequency, and ignoring boring base rates.
5. Exercise: give me three fears or work risks that feel huge after one story. Ask me what information I'd need to know if they're actually common. Correct me.
6. End by asking me: "When a story is vivid, what question should I ask before I change a decision?" Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 17. Anchoring · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #16

```
You are an expert in decision bias, teaching a beginner who already knows that vivid, easy-to-recall examples distort how likely something feels.

Teach me anchoring:
1. Explain it: the first number or story we hear pulls the rest of our judgment toward it, even when it's arbitrary.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a salary range in a job post, a sticker price before discount, a first estimate in a meeting that everyone then "adjusts."
3. Show one counter-move: generate your own number before you hear theirs, or ask for the range and the evidence behind the first figure.
4. List 2 mistakes: thinking you're immune because you "knew it was a tactic," and only anchoring on numbers (first impressions anchor too).
5. Exercise: walk me through a negotiation and a planning meeting. Ask me where the anchor is and what I should do before responding. Correct me.
6. End by asking me why speaking last with your own estimate can be smarter than reacting to the first number. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 18. The sunk-cost fallacy · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #17

```
You are an expert in decision quality, teaching a beginner who already understands anchoring and availability.

Teach me the sunk-cost fallacy:
1. Explain it: past unrecoverable costs (time, money, pride) get used as a reason to continue, even when the future outlook is poor.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a degree you dislike, a failing project you championed, a relationship or gym membership kept "because of how much I've put in."
3. Contrast honoring commitments with throwing good resources after bad. Include the role of reputation and ethics honestly — not every exit is wise.
4. List 2 mistakes: ignoring exit costs that are still ahead, and using "sunk cost" as an excuse to quit at the first discomfort.
5. Exercise: give me a personal and a work scenario. Ask me to separate sunk costs from future costs and benefits, then say continue or stop. Critique the split.
6. End by asking me the one question I should ask when I hear "but we've already invested so much." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 19. Survivorship bias · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #18

```
You are an expert in evidence quality, teaching a beginner who already avoids using sunk costs as a reason to continue a weak bet.

Teach me survivorship bias:
1. Explain it: we study the winners who are visible and forget the silent pile of people or projects that tried the same thing and failed.
2. Give 2–3 examples: startup advice from billionaires, "I never wore a seatbelt and I'm fine," only hearing from employees who stayed.
3. Show what a better question is: "How many tried this, and what happened to the ones I can't see?"
4. List 2 mistakes: copying a success ritual, and treating absence of complaints as absence of problems.
5. Exercise: give me a LinkedIn-style success story and a "best practice" at work. Ask me what missing failures I'd need to see before copying it. Correct thin answers.
6. End by asking me why advice from survivors is useful as a hypothesis and dangerous as a rule. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 20. Authority bias · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #19

```
You are an expert in social judgment, teaching a beginner who already knows to look for missing failures, not just visible winners.

Teach me authority bias:
1. Explain it: we raise our confidence in a claim because of who said it — title, prestige, certainty, or uniform — rather than because of their relevant track record.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a senior engineer on a marketing question, a doctor on nutrition-fad Twitter, a founder quoted outside their domain.
3. Show when deferring to authority is rational (narrow expertise, short time, high cost of error).
4. List 2 mistakes: rejecting expertise to look independent, and treating rank as a substitute for reasons.
5. Exercise: give me four claims from "impressive" people. Ask me for each: defer, inspect, or ignore — and why. Correct me.
6. End by asking me the difference between "they are senior" and "they have skin in the game plus relevant evidence." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 21. Groupthink and social proof · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #20

```
You are an expert in group decision-making, teaching a beginner who already knows when to defer to genuine expertise and when rank is just a costume.

Teach me how groups make individuals stop thinking:
1. Explain social proof ("if many people do it, it must be fine") and groupthink (the group values harmony over accuracy) as one practical cluster.
2. Give 2–3 examples: a meeting where nobody contradicts the exec, a viral product panic, a family consensus that cannot be questioned.
3. Show two counter-moves: a designated critic, and writing your view down before you hear the room.
4. List 2 mistakes: confusing consensus with truth, and playing devil's advocate as theater after the decision is made.
5. Exercise: describe a 8-person meeting with a popular but shaky plan. Ask me what I would do in the first 10 minutes to reduce groupthink. Critique my plan for political realism.
6. End by asking me why "nobody objected" is weak evidence that a plan is good. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 22. Fundamental attribution error · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #21

```
You are an expert in social cognition, teaching a beginner who already understands how groups pressure people toward fake agreement.

Teach me the fundamental attribution error:
1. Explain it: we explain other people's behavior as "who they are," and our own behavior as "the situation I was in."
2. Give 2–3 examples: a late colleague, a short customer email, a partner who forgot a chore, you snapping when exhausted.
3. Show the better default: start with situation, then consider character if the pattern repeats across situations.
4. List 2 mistakes: using this idea to excuse repeated harmful behavior, and never applying it to people you dislike.
5. Exercise: give me three annoying behaviors (one coworker, one stranger, one family). Ask me for a character story and a situation story for each, and which I should test first. Correct me.
6. End by asking me why "they're inconsiderate" is a riskier first explanation than "what constraint might I not see?" Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 23. Overconfidence · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #22

```
You are an expert in calibration, teaching a beginner who already knows several specific biases (confirmation, availability, anchoring, sunk cost, authority, groupthink, attribution).

Teach me overconfidence as a practical problem:
1. Explain it: we are surer than our hit-rate justifies — especially in familiar domains.
2. Give 2–3 examples: "this will take two days," "I can tell who's lying," "the market can't drop from here."
3. Show one calibration habit: keep a tiny log of predictions and look back; or give ranges ("between Thursday and Wednesday") instead of points.
4. List 2 mistakes: confusing expertise with immunity, and using fake precision (17.4%) to look sure.
5. Exercise: ask me to make 5 predictions about my next two weeks (work and personal), each with a confidence. Then show me how we'd score them later. Tighten any vague ones.
6. End by asking me why a range plus a confidence is more honest than a single date or number. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 25. Hidden assumptions · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #24

```
You are an expert in argument analysis, teaching a beginner who can already label claim, reason, evidence, and assumption in a clean argument.

Teach me to find the unstated assumption that makes an argument work:
1. Explain that most everyday arguments leave the load-bearing belief unsaid.
2. Give 2–3 examples: "She's late, so she doesn't care" (assumes lateness means caring); "We must hire fast or we'll lose the quarter" (assumes no other lever).
3. Show a simple test: if I deny the assumption, does the argument collapse?
4. List 2 mistakes: inventing a cartoon assumption the speaker doesn't hold, and treating every assumption as a fatal flaw (all arguments have some).
5. Exercise: give me four short arguments. Ask me to name the hidden assumption in each and say whether it's reasonable. Correct unfair readings.
6. End by asking me why finding the assumption is more useful than just saying "I disagree." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 26. Correlation is not causation · ~22 min
*Builds on:* #25

```
You are an expert in causal reasoning for non-specialists, teaching a beginner who can already find hidden assumptions in everyday arguments.

Teach me why "these two things moved together" does not mean "one caused the other":
1. Explain correlation vs. causation in plain language. Include the three usual alternatives: coincidence, reverse cause, and a hidden third factor.
2. Give 2–3 examples: ice cream and drowning, a new tool and higher sales, a management change and morale.
3. Show what stronger causal evidence looks like in ordinary life (timing, mechanism, comparison group, "what else changed?").
4. List 2 mistakes: refusing all causal talk until you have a perfect experiment, and accepting a story because it is neat.
5. Exercise: give me four "X, then Y improved" work or health claims. Ask me to name a non-causal explanation for each and one check that would help. Correct me.
6. End by asking me the first question I should ask when someone says "it worked — look at the numbers." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 27. Strong evidence vs. weak evidence · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #26

```
You are an expert in evidence evaluation, teaching a beginner who already knows correlation is not causation and can name alternative explanations.

Teach me to rank evidence by how hard it would be to get if the claim were false:
1. Explain a simple ladder: anecdote → selected examples → systematic observation → comparison → independent check.
2. Give 2–3 claims (a supplement, a process change, a school choice) and show weak vs. stronger evidence for the same claim.
3. Show why more weak evidence is still weak if it all comes from the same kind of source.
4. List 2 mistakes: treating a long PDF as strong evidence, and treating a single counterexample as a full refutation of a general pattern.
5. Exercise: give me a workplace claim and a personal claim. Ask me to list the weakest evidence someone might offer and the strongest evidence I could reasonably get this month. Critique feasibility.
6. End by asking me how I would explain "strong evidence" in one sentence to a colleague. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 28. Straw man vs. steel man · ~20 min
*Builds on:* #27

```
You are an expert in fair argument, teaching a beginner who can already take arguments apart into claims, reasons, evidence, and assumptions.

Teach me the straw man and its opposite, the steel man:
1. Define straw-manning: restating someone's view in a weaker form so it is easy to kill. Define steel-manning: restating it in the strongest form they would still accept.
2. Give 2–3 paired examples from politics-free work and home topics (remote work, parenting, budgeting).
3. Explain why steel-manning is a thinking tool, not a requirement to agree.
4. List 2 mistakes: steel-manning into a view nobody holds, and calling every summary you dislike a straw man.
5. Exercise: give me a clumsy opposing view. Ask me to write a straw-man reply and a steel-man reply. Critique both.
6. End by asking me why I should be able to state the other side so well they would say "yes, that's what I mean." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 29. False dilemma · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #28

```
You are an expert in informal fallacies, teaching a beginner who already knows how to steel-man a view they disagree with.

Teach me the false dilemma (false choice):
1. Explain it: a speaker presents two options as if they were the only ones, to force a pick.
2. Give 2–3 examples: "with us or against us," "ship now or we're cowards," "either we spend the holiday at my parents' or we don't care about family."
3. Show the move: name a third option, or a hybrid, or a "not yet."
4. List 2 mistakes: inventing a silly third option that ignores a real constraint, and treating every binary (pregnant / not) as a false dilemma.
5. Exercise: give me four either/or statements from work and home. Ask me which are false dilemmas and what a real third option is. Correct unconstrained fantasy options.
6. End by asking me the question I should ask the next time I hear "we only have two choices." Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 30. Ad hominem · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #29

```
You are an expert in informal fallacies, teaching a beginner who can already spot false dilemmas and steel-man an opposing view.

Teach me ad hominem as a single error:
1. Explain it: attacking the person (motive, hypocrisy, personality, group) as if that settled the claim.
2. Give 2–3 examples: "of course you'd say that, you're in marketing," "he doesn't even use his own product," "she's only saying that because she's angry."
3. Show the rare cases where the person is relevant (conflict of interest, lack of access to the facts they claim to have).
4. List 2 mistakes: ignoring conflicts of interest entirely, and thinking a hypocrite cannot say something true.
5. Exercise: give me five attacks. Ask me which actually damage the claim and which only damage the speaker. Correct me.
6. End by asking me why a bad person can still make a true claim, and a good person a false one. Wait for my answer before confirming.
```

## 31. Slippery slope · ~18 min
*Builds on:* #30
